title: Delivered end-to-end independently
---

A **behavioral question** asks for a real example from your past work: "Tell me about a time you…". The interviewer uses how you acted then to judge how you will act in the role, because past situations reveal patterns of behavior. Each question targets one **competency**, a way of working the role needs, such as ownership or learning quickly. The interviewer scores your answer against a description of what a weak and a strong example of that competency look like. The six stories in G7 cover the six competencies that goal.md draws from the job description.

**STAR** is a structure for telling one example so the interviewer hears every part they score:

- **Situation** — the context: where you worked, the project, what made it hard. Just enough for the rest to make sense.
- **Task** — your goal or responsibility in it: what you, personally, had to achieve.
- **Action** — the steps you took, and why you chose them. This is the part being scored, so it gets most of the time: about 60% of the answer, against about 20% for the Situation and 10% each for Task and Result.
- **Result** — what happened because of your actions, with a number where one exists, and what you learned.

Three rules hold for every story:

- **Say "I", not "we".** The interviewer is hiring you, so they need your part of the team's work. Name the team once, then say what you did.
- **One real occasion.** "I usually…" is a generalization and gives nothing to score; a single specific situation does.
- **A failure counts.** A story that went wrong works, as long as what you learned from it is clear.

Stories are picked in advance and never scripted: a memorized script sounds recited, so keep the facts ready and tell them in your own words each time. A story is **picked** once you can name its situation in one sentence and recall what you did, step by step. Stories come from jobs, side projects, or study; the capstone in goal.md, the AI assistant this study builds to prove G1–G6, can supply one where work cannot. Use six different stories, one per competency. If one story fits two competencies, use it for the one it fits best.

**What this question tests.** **End-to-end delivery** means owning a piece of work from an unclear start to a result someone uses: shaping the problem, building it, shipping it, and following up after release. The job description asks for someone who works independently and builds production-ready AI applications (goal.md). In a delivery organization, a consulting firm that builds projects for clients, that means taking a client's need and finishing it without a manager steering each step. The interviewer listens for ownership: decisions you made yourself, gaps nobody assigned that you filled, and an ending in real use.

Independent work still includes asking. The strongest version also shows the one decision you took to someone else, and why (G7.2).

**How it is asked:**

- "Tell me about a project you owned from start to finish."
- "Describe a time you delivered something with little guidance."
- "Walk me through something you built and shipped on your own."

**Questions to find your story:**

- What have I built that someone else now uses: a service, a pipeline, a tool, a report?
- Where did I start with only a vague goal and define the scope myself?
- Which part of a project had nobody on it until I took it: the data, the deployment, the tests, the docs?
- When did I make a decision that would normally go to a lead, and did it hold?
- What went wrong midway, and how did I recover?
- Can I show that it ended: a release, a handover, a user, a number?

**A strong pick** covers several stages (problem, build, ship, follow-up), has a real obstacle, and has an end you can show. A story where someone else made the key decisions answers a different question.
